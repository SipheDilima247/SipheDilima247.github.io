Global growth resilience amid policy divergence

August 18, 2026 • 8 min read

Despite tighter financial conditions in several advanced economies, recent data from the IMF and national accounts suggest underlying demand remains more durable than many models predicted.

The first half of 2026 has produced a familiar tension: policy rates remain elevated in real terms across much of the developed world, yet activity indicators have not deteriorated as sharply as historical relationships would imply. This piece examines the main sources of resilience and the implications for the second half of the year.

What the data are showing

Global GDP growth estimates from the IMF’s most recent updates have been revised modestly higher for 2026 relative to projections made six months earlier. The upward revisions are concentrated in the United States and parts of Asia, while the euro area and the United Kingdom remain more subdued.

Key supporting factors include:

  • Household balance sheets that, on aggregate, remain healthier than pre-pandemic norms in several large economies
  • Continued strength in services activity, particularly in the United States
  • Fiscal support that has not been withdrawn as rapidly as some market participants expected
  • A gradual easing in supply-side constraints that had previously amplified inflationary pressures

Policy divergence and financial conditions

Central banks are no longer moving in lockstep. While some have begun carefully adjusting policy, others remain on hold or are still assessing the lag effects of earlier tightening. This divergence is already visible in currency markets and cross-border capital flows.

Research from the BIS has repeatedly highlighted that the transmission of monetary policy is neither uniform nor instantaneous. The current environment appears consistent with that view: financial conditions have tightened in rate-sensitive sectors, yet broader demand has proved more elastic than expected.

Risks to the outlook

Resilience does not equal invulnerability. The main risks remain:

  • A sharper-than-expected slowdown in labour demand that feeds back into consumption
  • Renewed inflationary pressures that force a reassessment of the policy path
  • Geopolitical or energy shocks that reintroduce supply constraints
  • Financial market stress that amplifies the impact of higher rates

At present, the baseline remains one of gradual moderation rather than abrupt contraction. That assessment will be updated as new labour market, inflation, and activity data arrive.
